01 / UNDERSTAND YOUR BODY

What Is Gestational Diabetes?

First things first: this is not your fault.
And you don’t have to figure it out alone.

LET’S KEEP IT SIMPLE

A change in how your body
manages blood sugar.

During pregnancy, hormones can make insulin work less effectively. If your body can’t make enough extra insulin, glucose (blood sugar) rises. This is called gestational diabetes.

It often has no noticeable symptoms, which is why screening—usually around 24–28 weeks—is important. Your care team may recommend earlier testing.

Learn more at NIDDK ↗ (opens in new tab)
01

Manageable, with support

Food, movement, glucose checks, and sometimes medication work together. Needing insulin is not a failure.

02

Small actions make a difference

Working with your team to manage glucose helps lower the chance of pregnancy and birth complications.

03

Your plan should fit you

Your needs, culture, appetite, and pregnancy matter. A registered dietitian can help you find your balance.

A companion, not a clinician. This website provides general education—not diagnosis or personalized treatment. Follow the food, activity, and glucose targets agreed with your pregnancy care team.

A note about targets

Common pregnancy targets are below 95 mg/dL (5.3 mmol/L) fasting, below 140 (7.8) at 1 hour, or below 120 (6.7) at 2 hours after starting a meal. Your team may set different targets.

This log doesn’t assess readings or suggest treatment. Follow your team’s instructions for high or low results. If you feel seriously unwell, seek urgent medical help.

06 / FEEL-GOOD MOVEMENT

Movement

A gentle start.
A little more energy.

If your pregnancy care team says it’s safe, gentle movement can support glucose management, mood, and sleep. A short walk after a meal is one place to begin.

☀❧Find your own pace.
01A comfortable walk5–10 minutes · easy pace+

Try a level route after a meal. Walk at a pace where you can talk comfortably. Start with a few minutes, bring water, and stop if you don’t feel well.

02A gentle movement break2–5 minutes · at home+

After sitting, stand slowly and try easy shoulder rolls or a short walk around the room. Use a chair for balance if needed. Avoid holding your breath or pushing through discomfort.

03Make it your ownA routine that fits your day+

Pregnancy-adapted stretching, swimming, or stationary cycling may be options if approved by your clinician. Build up gradually; activity recommendations depend on your pregnancy and health.

♡

Listen to your body. Stop and contact your care team for dizziness, chest pain, bleeding, fluid leakage, or painful contractions. If you use insulin, ask how to prevent and manage low glucose during activity.

07 / CARE BEYOND THE BUMP

After Delivery

Your care matters in this next chapter, too.

01 / THE EARLY DAYS

A new rhythm

Gestational diabetes often resolves after birth, but not always. Your team will advise you on glucose checks and changes to medicine. Don’t change treatment on your own.

02 / AT 4–12 WEEKS

Make time for a follow-up

Ask about a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test 4–12 weeks after delivery. It checks whether your blood sugar has returned to its usual range—even if you feel well.

03 / THE LONGER VIEW

Keep caring for you

A history of gestational diabetes raises future type 2 diabetes risk. Arrange repeat screening every 1–3 years and tell your team before another pregnancy.

♡

Rest, regular nourishment, and support all count. If you choose to breastfeed, ask for feeding support and advice about any medicines.
